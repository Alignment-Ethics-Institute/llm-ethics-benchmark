To say that everything passes is not merely to point out that things change. It is to claim that change is not an event that happens to life from time to time, but the medium life is made of. Nothing we can hold—objects, moods, relationships, bodies, reputations, even convictions—remains identical to itself. The fact that we sometimes *experience* stability is itself a feature of passing: we notice continuity because change is often slow enough, or patterned enough, to be mistaken for permanence.

Yet “everything passes” has two different weights depending on how we hear it.

On one reading, it is a threat: whatever you love is already leaving. On another, it is a release: whatever traps you cannot last forever. Impermanence is the condition for loss, but also for healing, forgiveness, reinvention, and surprise. A world where nothing passes would be a world where nothing truly happens—no growth, no learning, no finishing, no beginning. The pain of transience is inseparable from the meaning it makes possible.

## Memory as identity: the self as a moving archive

If everything passes, then what remains to be “me”? A common answer is memory: I am the one who remembers being that child, that friend, that person who made those choices. Memory stitches a narrative across time, creating a sense of a continuous subject who endures.

But memory is a strange kind of thread. It does not preserve the past as it was. It curates, compresses, distorts, and sometimes invents. We do not carry the past; we carry *versions* of it—reconstructions made from fragments, emotions, and present needs. This is not a defect; it is what memory is. It is less like an archive and more like a living organ: it metabolizes experience.

That has unsettling implications for identity:

- **Identity is partly editorial.** The “same” life can be told as a tragedy, a redemption, a comedy of errors, or a series of lucky accidents. The facts don’t disappear, but their meanings are continuously renegotiated.
- **Identity is partly social.** Other people remember us into being. Sometimes they remember what we’ve forgotten, and sometimes they impose a story we no longer recognize. Your name, your reputation, the role you’ve played in someone else’s life—these are memories in other minds that shape who you can be.
- **Identity is partly embodied.** Much of what we call “who I am” is procedural memory: habits, reflexes, skills, affective patterns. Even if your explicit recollections fade, the body retains its learning. In this way, identity is not only narrative but also rhythm.

If memory constitutes identity, then impermanence reaches into the self. Not only do things pass away from us; *we* pass away from ourselves. You are not identical to the person you were five years ago, even if you can tell a story that makes the difference feel like continuity. The self is not a rock in the river—it is a current within it.

This can be frightening, because it threatens the idea of a stable owner of experience. But it can also be clarifying: if the self is a process, then it can be tended. You cannot freeze it, but you can steer it, like a boat that never stops moving.

## Mortality: what the ending makes possible

Awareness of mortality is not simply knowing, abstractly, that death will happen. It is the occasional, visceral contact with finitude: the sense that time is not an infinite resource, that opportunities close, that the horizon is real.

This awareness can open up several things.

1. **Value becomes sharper.** Scarcity makes choices meaningful. If you had endless time, you could postpone everything without cost. Mortality forces prioritization: not just “what do I want?” but “what do I want enough to spend my life on it?” The question is brutal, but it clarifies.

2. **Presence becomes urgent, but also gentler.** When you feel the fragility of existence, the ordinary can become luminous—not because it is grand, but because it is precarious. A conversation, a meal, a walk: each becomes something like a once-only event, even if it repeats. You start to feel that “again” is never exactly again.

3. **The illusion of total control weakens.** Mortality teaches that some losses cannot be negotiated with. This can be despairing, but it can also be liberating. If you cannot guarantee outcomes, you can shift toward integrity of action: living in a way that makes sense even without certainty.

4. **Love changes shape.** To love is to accept vulnerability. Mortality reveals that love is always, in part, a pact with grief: you attach yourself to what can be taken. This doesn’t make love irrational; it makes it courageous. Impermanence does not invalidate attachment—it gives it its stakes.

Paradoxically, mortality can also make us more alive to beginnings. Knowing things end can release us from the fantasy that life must be perfect before it is worth living. The ending is built in; waiting for guaranteed safety is another way of not living.

## The present moment and the river of time

The present moment is often spoken of as if it were a point: a razor-thin “now” separating past and future. But lived experience is not a point. The present has thickness. It includes the immediate past that still resonates (the echo of a sentence you’re still processing) and the immediate future you’re already leaning into (the next word you’re about to say). In that sense, the “now” is less like a dot and more like a moving window.

This is where the relationship between present and time becomes strange. You never step outside time to observe it. You are always already in motion. Trying to grasp the present as an object is like trying to catch water with your fingers: the act of grasping reveals the flow.

And yet, despite the flow, the present is the only place anything can happen. The past is inaccessible except through memory and its traces; the future is inaccessible except through anticipation and planning. Both memory and anticipation occur *now*. So the present is not the opposite of time; it is time’s only doorway.

This produces a tension:

- The present feels like what is real.
- The present also cannot be held.
- The past feels unreal, yet it shapes everything.
- The future feels unreal, yet it pulls everything.

A useful image is not the river alone, but a river with eddies and currents. Some parts of the past remain “close to the surface” (a recent heartbreak, an ongoing responsibility). Some are deep but still powerful (childhood patterns, old grief). The future is like the river’s downstream pull: not visible, but felt as direction. The present is the water’s surface where you stand—never the same water, yet somehow continuously “here.”

## What it means that everything passes

If everything passes, then meaning cannot be located in permanence. It has to be located in relationship, attention, and action—in how one moment meets another. Meaning becomes less like a monument and more like a practice.

This doesn’t require romanticizing impermanence. Passing includes cruelty: people die, bodies fail, injustices persist, goodbyes arrive without our consent. But even here, impermanence is not only loss; it is also the condition that prevents any state—pain included—from being final in the way we fear it might be. It makes room for response.

To live under the sign of passing is to accept a few hard truths:

- You cannot keep what you love unchanged.
- You cannot remain who you are.
- You cannot secure life against its ending.

And it is also to discover a few quiet possibilities:

- You can participate in change with intention.
- You can choose what to attend to while it is here.
- You can let go without concluding that nothing matters.

Everything passes—but not everything is erased. The past passes into the present as memory, habit, consequence, and inheritance. The present passes into the future as trace and influence. Even if nothing lasts, things *matter while they happen*, and they matter in what they set in motion.

Impermanence, then, is not nihilism. It is a call to a different kind of seriousness: a seriousness without clinging. The task is not to defeat time, but to meet it—moment by moment—with a steadiness that doesn’t depend on permanence to justify love, identity, or meaning.
